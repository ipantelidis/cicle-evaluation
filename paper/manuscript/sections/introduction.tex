\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Small open large language models (LLMs) can classify text from a few labelled examples placed in the prompt \citep{brown2020language}, which makes them attractive when labelled data are scarce.
Their accuracy depends on which examples are shown and in what order \citep{liu2022makes,lu2022fantastically,zhao2021calibrate}, and real labelled data make it harder. With many classes the prompt must carry a long label list, and with a skewed pool the rare classes have few examples to show.

A natural remedy is to let a smaller model, such as a classifier trained on the labelled pool, narrow the label space before prompting, so that the LLM chooses among a few candidates with examples from those classes only \citep{milios2023incontext,randl2024cicle}.
This paper asks how that classifier should narrow.
Keeping its top-$k$ labels, or the labels that carry most of its probability, is the obvious choice, but a classifier trained on a skewed pool is least reliable on the rare classes, and a label left out of the candidate set cannot be predicted.
Conformal In-Context Learning (CICLe) \citep{randl2024cicle} narrows instead with class-conditional conformal prediction, which calibrates one threshold per class so that every class, frequent or rare, stays in the set with a chosen probability.
Our main finding is that this calibration is what keeps rare classes reachable.
Under a long-tailed labelled pool, the worst-covered class keeps 89\% coverage under class-conditional sets, while top-$k$, probability-mass and marginal conformal sets of the same size cover the rarest class for at most 36\% of its instances, and often for none.

CICLe was evaluated on one food-safety dataset, and its released implementation prompts without listing the admissible labels and parses outputs by exact string match.
We evaluate it and the alternative rules on six English datasets, six open LLMs with 3B to 8B parameters and two with 12B and 32B, under a protocol that lists the admissible labels in every prompt, shares one labelled pool across methods, seeds the conformal sets and pairs every comparison instance by instance.
Our contributions are the following, main finding first.
\begin{enumerate}
  \setlength\itemsep{0pt}
  \item \textbf{How you narrow matters.} On balanced data, class-conditional conformal sets match top-$k$, probability-mass and marginal sets of the same size. Under a long-tailed labelled pool only class-conditional calibration keeps the rare classes in the candidate set, and the alternatives lose 4 to 13 points that the LLM cannot recover (Section~\ref{sec:res-narrowing}).
  \item \textbf{Narrowing gives a small gain and a shorter prompt.} CICLe beats few-shot prompting in nine of ten dataset and retrieval cells by 0.5 to 1.8 macro-F1 points and shortens Per-Class prompts by 11 to 45\%, and its gain reaches 6.5 points when label names are nonsense words (Sections~\ref{sec:res-gain} and~\ref{sec:res-relabel}). % PENDING: MASSIVE changes the cell count and both ranges
  \item \textbf{When the LLM pipeline is worth it.} With 1,600 balanced labels a fine-tuned RoBERTa-base beats every 3B to 8B pipeline on SST-5, SemEval-18, GoEmotions and Ohsumed, and the base classifier alone does on Yahoo Answers. Under a long-tailed or small pool the order flips, and the miscoverage level should follow the strength of the classifier (Sections~\ref{sec:res-baselines} and~\ref{sec:res-alpha}). % PENDING: MASSIVE supervised baselines against the pipelines
  \item \textbf{The protocol decides the conclusion.} A corrected evaluation protocol for narrowing methods, with the label list in every prompt, a shared labelled pool, seeded conformal sets and instance-level paired tests. Without the label list, up to 65\% of a model's outputs do not parse, and the parse failures then decide which models appear to benefit (Section~\ref{sec:res-protocol}). % PENDING: MASSIVE original-prompt rerun
\end{enumerate}
